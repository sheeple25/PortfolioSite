A 300-page documentation of bamboo craft in Vietnam, made by fourteen of us from CEPT under Dr. Rebecca Reubens.

Fourteen days across North and Central Vietnam, talking to village artisans and to larger manufacturers. The aim wasn't to record the craft. It was to find practices that could be brought back to India, to strengthen or start industries here.

These are the sections I contributed.

## The Conical Hat

No object is more Vietnamese than the *nón lá*. Woven primarily from palm leaves, sometimes straw, bamboo or mortar. It dates back over 3,000 years, and it is still everywhere — in Hanoi's traffic, and on every cyclist in the countryside.

It is waterproof and light enough that it never has to come off outdoors. Its draft angle means the hats stack.

One name covers many typologies. The flat, round *nón ba tầm* for rural women at festivities. The Huế poem hats. The *nón ngựa*, worn on horseback.

Today they're used daily and, increasingly, decoratively — patterns printed or embroidered onto the leaves, sometimes over a canvas layer added for printability and structure.

What makes it quintessential is fit. It suits the conditions, habits and practices of the people who wear it so exactly that it has become symbolic far beyond the object.

## Phú Vinh

Nearly 400 years old, and known for bamboo and rattan handicrafts.

There are two accounts of how the craft began. One has villagers on a beach called Gò Đậu making hats from fallen stork feathers, turning to bamboo and rattan when the feathers ran out. The other has the village surrounded by water, building bamboo boats to move through monsoon, until a visiting Queen funded the settlement and taught them to work the material.

## Material and Method

Four bamboos are used — *tre*, *giang*, *nứa*, *nùng*. Giang is the common one, for flexibility and availability.

Rattan, *mây*, is a vine. It grows up to a metre a year to a maximum of five metres before harvest. Timing matters: too young and it's over-flexible, too old and it's brittle.

The process, for a coaster:

**Design first**, which sets form, material and technique.

**Split by hand** into strips from 1mm to 5cm, each thickness for a different job. Soak them, or they turn brittle.

**Weave while the moisture is still in.** Thick pieces get bent into concentric rings for a framework; thinner strips are woven through the rings and through holes in the ceramic centre, binding it all together.

**Soak in glue and water** to stiffen, where the product needs it.

**Colour last.** Smoke from *cây si* leaves gives black. Straw smoke gives yellows. Powder dye gives pinks and limes.

Then the *công cụ nện* — a tamper. Struck against the surface for around thirty seconds, it closes the gaps and air bubbles between woven members and seats any ceramic component flush.

## The Workshop

We spoke with Mr. Nguyễn Văn Tịnh and his son Mr. Nguyễn Phương Quang at Việt Quang Rattan and Bamboo Wickerwork.

Raw bamboo comes from Quảng Ngãi or Quảng Nam, bundled and stacked into trucks. It needs no packaging — the material is durable enough to travel bare.

The workshop hires from around the village. Each person makes four or five products a day, working into the night. Machines are used only for the initial splitting.

Orders arrive through Facebook advertisements and word of mouth. Most output goes to hospitality — decoration and thematic fixtures — so demand is seasonal, and the festive-season gift basket is the big one. Each workshop operates independently, often serving one country.

The products are intricate and still repairable: broken ends are split and rejoined so the patch disappears.

## What Was Worth Bringing Home

**Nothing is forced onto the material.** No design in the village fights what bamboo and rattan want to do. Flexibility is controlled by varying the thickness and number of members; strength comes from the weave itself. Member thickness is always proportionate to the surface being made.

**One technique, many products.** Rattan wound around a central ceramic disk becomes a coaster, a bag, or a tray depending only on scale. Output shifts with the season — gift baskets, then tourist goods, then household products. One learned technique, redeployed, keeps both labour and material fully used.

**Craft can reach scale without becoming mass production.** By training a stable team of skilled workers and breaking the process into discrete steps, these workshops fill orders in the hundreds, domestically and internationally, with the work still done by hand.

That last one is the finding. This division-of-skilled-labour approach is atypical in India, and it is the most direct lever I saw for reviving crafts that are dying here.

## What I Learned

How to ask the right questions, and how to structure an interview so the answers are usable.

Fourteen days is not long. What made it productive was going in with the comparison already framed — not *how is this made*, but *what does this place do that ours doesn't*.
