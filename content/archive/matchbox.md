A bedside lamp that has to give both directed and ambient light, in a form that could be mass-manufactured.

## Light Along a Surface

The interest was in the quality of light — specifically how it changes when cast *along* a surface rather than at one.

That question drove the ideation, starting in 1:5 cardboard, because it isn't a question you can answer by drawing.

## Sixty Degrees

Once the form settled, Keyshot answered the one number that mattered: how the shade's angle changes the throw.

Iterating landed on 60° to the horizontal. At that angle the light rakes across the shade's surface instead of washing it, and it appears to move as you move.

## Adjustable, Not Binary

An on/off lamp gives you one condition. A lamp with a range gives you the room at several times of night.

The shade slides to three fixed heights on a simple magnetic locking mechanism, verified along with the light throw on a 1:1 K-line model.

Shade down is directed light for reading. Shade up is ambient.

## The Holes

The final prototype is 5mm plywood, and its two rows of holes each do a job.

**On the back**, three rows mark the three heights the shade sets to — so the adjustment tells you where it is without any labelling.

**On the front**, holes let some light escape even with the shade fully shut. The lamp never goes fully dark, which is what makes it usable as a bedside object rather than a task light that happens to be near a bed.

## Packaging

1.5mm cardboard, with a handle built into it.

The base folds in the manner of a Chinese takeout box, so the lamp's own weight holds it shut. No glue, no closure to fail.

## What I Learned

How to iterate through models instead of sketches, and how to work with something as intangible as light.

Sketching couldn't have found 60°. Only building it at scale and looking at it could.
