A research studio on how technology mediates romantic relationships.

I looked around and noticed that people are lonely. Dating apps exist. People don't like them, and they don't work.

The question was why both are true at once.

## Three Failures

Dating apps fail on three separate axes, and each has its own evidence.

**Functional.** They don't work. 80% of users are men. A 1.96% match rate. 66% say app conversations went nowhere.

**Experiential.** People don't like using them. 78% report burnout. 46% report medium-to-high dissatisfaction. 50% find them embarrassing.

**Structural.** They don't want to get you matches. 62% of revenue is subscriptions. Tinder took $1B from active users in 2024. 75% say apps aren't for real relationships.

The third failure explains the first two. A product paid by the month has no reason to end your need for it.

In short, current solutions are high pressure, low trust, and detached from real life.

## Research

Sixteen interviews, ages 20–25, on how people actually find each other.

Then a matchmaking experiment: fourteen students across the CEPT campus, around 400 discrete data points, three matches. They told me their fears and their biggest flexes. That experiment is what proved value-based matching was the only viable basis for an intervention.

## What Came Back

**Legitimacy and stigma.** Apps read as less legitimate than meeting organically. Older generations reject them as a real starting point. Women face sharper policing than men — gossip, the "desperate" label, rumours after a rejection.

**Communication.** First messages felt high-stakes and hard. Sustaining a conversation was harder. Text flattens tone, so intent is unreadable. Women on Bumble carried the initiation burden.

**Authenticity.** Projection is expected — polished photos, curated prompts, filters. Authenticity gets judged through effort, candids and specific prompts, rather than through anything essential.

**Platforms.** Instagram is where romance starts. WhatsApp is where it continues, rarely where it begins. Snapchat was almost universally dismissed as unsafe.

**Expectations.** Few saw apps as a route to something serious. The stated motivations were companionship and casual exploration. Some had reframed apps away from romance entirely — networking, broadening circles, meeting interesting people.

And the finding that mattered most: **apps blur motivation.** Even when someone says companionship, the structure funnels it into a romantic or sexual frame. Many found that inauthentic.

**Safety and control.** Women wanted filtering, blocking, and the ability to reject without danger. Men framed vulnerability as invisibility — the difficulty of standing out at all.

## Nine Archetypes

Mapped against five axes: intent to meet in real life, clarity of goal, effort put in, trust in the apps, and risk posed to others.

**The Validation Seeker** — craves attention, no intent of going offline. **The Mastikhor** — there for thrill and mischief. **The Point Nemo** — soothing loneliness or heartbreak. **The Horndog** — upfront about sex. **The Mole** — friendships and networks, no romance. **The Goody Two Shoes** — genuine intent, no traction. **The Escapist** — engaged until it gets heavy, then gone. **The Bad Actor** — hostile, exploitative, manipulative. **The Apostate** — ex-power users who quit after burnout.

The archetypes matter because behaviour is not private here. Any user's behaviour lands on other users.

Some pairs are a zero-sum game. The Validation Seeker getting compliments with no intent to meet is, by the same action, the Goody Two Shoes being denied the thing they came for. One person's satisfied need is another's wasted month.

## Two Different Problems

POEM analysis split the cohorts cleanly.

**For women (19–25):** the apps aren't fun. Something as anticipated as romance should be enjoyable, and the process is stressful at every stage. Stigma and gendered moral policing. Apps that trivialise and gamify. Anxiety, uncertainty, pressure, and no natural flow. What they valued was control — blocking, filtering, safety features, and a buffer before meeting.

**For men (20–24):** the gender imbalance is the whole problem. It produces competition, a felt need to be performative, and a self-esteem hit for the majority who struggle to get matches. Profile creation is experienced as the central struggle. What they valued was that a mutual match is pre-qualified interest, and that it's easier to approach than real life.

## The House Always Wins

The iceberg model pushed past behaviour to cause. Underneath sits the capitalist design of the apps — swipe culture, gamification, paid tiers — driving superficiality and manufactured scarcity, and human motives refracted through it: belonging, status, validation.

The synthesis is a closed loop with three parts.

**The Masquerade.** Everyone wears masks. Curated profiles, roles played, other people treated as less than fully real.

**The Casino.** A broken slot machine. Moods and impulses keep you pulling, and the house always wins.

**The Sword of Damocles.** Judgement hangs over all of it. Fear of exposure pushes people to stay masked — which feeds the masquerade, which feeds the odds, which feeds the rejection.

Users feel the need to perform, so they curate. They play a rigged game. They get judged. Being judged makes them perform harder.

## The Intervention

Augment how relationships actually begin. Don't replace it.

Technology as scaffold, not substitute. Three requirements: it augments real life, it's fun to experience, and it makes starting easier.

**Traces is a way of connecting, low pressure, in the real world.**

**No swiping.** People aren't meant to have the population of their city at their fingertips. Less choice, more intention.

**No profiles.** Real people don't fit in six photos, three prompts and some interest tags. Less curation.

**No cold starts.** Conversations start and end with "hey" and "wyd". You deserve better.

## How It Works

A **Trace** is a piece of you. A photo, a note, a drawing, a song, a film — any media that catches a feeling you can't put into words.

A **Situation** is a game of sorts: a hypothetical where your choices say who you are. No right answers. You only have to answer as yourself, so the system can match you with people you'll actually get on with.

You either play a Situation or upload a Trace. Based on the personality your answers describe, you're matched with people on your wavelength, and you see their Traces gradually, in AR.

Nobody sends you likes. Your Traces go out to compatible people, and theirs come to you.

If you like someone's Trace — or they like yours — there are two ways forward. **Request a date**, straight to the point. Or **request a co-op**, a collaborative activity that builds chemistry before you meet.

## Feature by Problem

Every feature answers something the research turned up.

**Profiles are low-effort and rushed** → *Traces*. Upload over time instead of building a profile in one sitting. Quality rises, pressure drops.

**Profiles don't feel like real people** → *View in AR*. Seeing someone's Traces accumulate over time makes the information feel like it came from a person.

**Matching algorithms don't work** → *Situations*. Difficult moral and value-based choices tell the system what kind of person you are, which is what makes value-based matching possible at all.

**Cold starts are high-stress** → *Co-op activities*. A shared goal instead of an empty chat box.

**Matches go nowhere** → *Date planning*.

**Solo dates carry too much pressure** → *Group dates*.
