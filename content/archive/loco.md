Indian Railways needed a new waterless urinal for the WAG-9 cargo and WAP-7 passenger locomotives. The existing unit is a sheet metal box that current pilots avoid using.

The brief singled out one group it had to work for: female loco pilots, whom the existing design does not account for at all.

## The Constraints

Five, and all of them hard.

**Waterless.** No supply to draw on.

**No storage.** Waste exhausts directly out; nothing can be held on board.

**90 days between services.** Between them, upkeep is the pilots' own job.

**Self-cleanable**, with whatever is on board.

**1050 × 600mm.** A retrofit into an envelope that already exists.

## Talking to the Pilots

We visited the Tughlaqabad loco shed, saw the current unit in place, and spoke to two female pilots. Both chose to stay anonymous and asked not to be quoted directly.

What they described was avoidance, not usage.

**They prefer not to go while moving.** Most squat or hover rather than make contact.

**They plan their water intake around the timetable.** Stops give 15–20 minutes; that window is the real design constraint, not the cabin.

**They carry nothing in.** Everything stays in the cabin, because there is nowhere to put it down.

**They prefer Indian-style pans** — fewer touchpoints — but find Western-style more usable when it is clean. Combination units they dislike outright.

**Nothing signals the unit is a urinal**, so there is no expectation of what state it will be in.

And when shown existing female waterless urinals, they were neutral. They couldn't relate to any of them.

## What They Actually Wanted

**Water, for menstrual sanitation.** The one place the waterless constraint genuinely conflicts with the user.

**Room to turn around.**

**No evidence of prior use.**

**Not to carry everything themselves** — water, pads, all of it.

**A basin and a dustbin.** Stated most strongly of everything on the list.

**A railing.**

## What's Wrong With the Current Unit

A sheet metal box on a raised platform. Light and sanitiser both placed where you can't reach them. Cramped to the point of claustrophobia, and too short for anyone above about 176cm.

The combination Indo-Western pan has footrests at the wrong width and height relative to the seat. It is not suited to menstrual needs.

It does fit its footprint. And there is wasted space beside the unit and above the deadweight that nobody had claimed.

Two problems sat underneath all of it.

**Maintainability.** Too many touchpoints, no water, and nowhere to keep cleaning materials — so filth accumulates across the full 90 days and the unit becomes less usable the longer it goes.

**Splashback.** Standing urination in a tight geometry, accumulating between cleans.

## The Move

The breakthrough was spatial, not mechanical.

The cabin layout allowed the lavatory envelope to extend above elbow height on one side. That reclaimed volume was what turned a claustrophobic box into something with air in it — which was the quality the pilots kept describing when asked how the space should feel.

Roominess wasn't a comfort feature here. It was the difference between a unit that gets used and one that gets planned around.

## What I Learned

How to design inside real constraints, and how to work with a real client.

The constraints didn't loosen. The site visit is what made them navigable — the difference between reading "90 days between services" in a requirements document and standing in the thing that has to survive it.
